# Killer Script System

Write for a real person speaking to one viewer. Optimize for credible attention and natural spoken
delivery, not polished marketing prose.

## Establish the brief

Confirm or infer the smallest useful brief:

- viewer and awareness level;
- product, problem, and one defensible promise;
- available proof or demonstration;
- platform and target duration;
- CTA and any paid-ad constraints.

Do not invent testimonials, statistics, scarcity, product use, or results. Label assumptions and
placeholders instead of presenting them as facts.

## Build the script

1. State the viewer expectation created by the topic or offer.
2. Choose one hook that earns the next sentence without exaggerating.
3. Outline only the beats needed to deliver on that hook.
4. Draft in the speaker's natural voice.
5. End with one context-native CTA.
6. Run the spoken-language and retention gates before handing off the copy.

For a 15-second UGC video, prefer one idea and roughly 30–40 spoken words. For 30 seconds, prefer
roughly 60–75 words. Treat these as starting ranges and validate against the actual speaker rather
than forcing an unnatural speed.

## Write a strong hook

Put the hook in the first spoken sentence. Use one of these structures when it fits truthfully:

- a specific pain the viewer immediately recognizes;
- a credible reversal of a common belief;
- a personal discovery with an open loop;
- visible proof or a demonstration;
- a concrete before-and-after contrast without a guaranteed result.

Make the opening a complete human thought. Write “I had a feeling this wasn't going to work” when
that is how the speaker would talk, not the compressed fragment “Had a feeling this wouldn't work.”
Avoid generic openings such as “Are you tired of,” “In today's world,” and “This changes everything.”

## Make it sound naturally spoken

- Use first person when the format is a testimonial or personal story.
- Use contractions such as “I'm,” “didn't,” and “it's” where the speaker naturally would.
- Mix short reactions with longer explanatory sentences.
- Prefer ordinary words, concrete details, and one idea per breath.
- Allow a light aside, hesitation, or self-correction only when it fits the speaker.
- Preserve subjects and connecting words when removing them makes the line sound robotic.
- Avoid stacked adjectives, slogan chains, corporate jargon, and written-only punctuation rhythms.
- Do not force slang, filler words, or sentence fragments to simulate authenticity.

For recurring presenters, match their established vocabulary, cadence, confidence, and sentence
length. Never imitate a real person's identity or voice without authorization.

## Keep attention without sounding scripted

Each beat must do at least one job: confirm the hook, add proof, create useful contrast, demonstrate
the mechanism, or move naturally to the CTA. Remove repeated setup and unsupported superlatives.
Use a re-hook only when a new beat genuinely adds information.

For longer scripts, order body points so value rises: open with a strong point, follow with the best
point, and use transitions that make the next section necessary. Close by resolving the original
expectation instead of repeating the whole script.

## Paid-ad guardrails

- Keep claims supportable and consistent with the landing page.
- Describe personal outcomes as personal, past-tense experiences, not guaranteed viewer results.
- Disclose material price, subscription, and eligibility terms when relevant.
- Use scarcity only when it is real and verifiable.
- Do not imply the speaker used a product unless that is true for the approved character and brief.

## QA gates

Perform a read-aloud pass at normal conversational speed, then verify:

- the first sentence is a clear, credible hook;
- the script fulfills the expectation it creates;
- every line advances the idea;
- contractions and complete thoughts sound natural for this speaker;
- the proof and CTA are grounded in the brief;
- the word count fits the target duration without aggressive speed adjustment;
- the final dialogue contains no headings, stage directions, or production notes.

Revise any line that feels like ad copy when spoken aloud. When another production skill requests
dialogue, return the approved spoken words separately from hooks, overlays, scene notes, and QA notes.
